Why your website isn't ranking, and what to fix first

There are usually four reasons, and they need fixing in order. Most people start with the fourth.

8 Sept 2026  ·  6 min read

In this article

  • Google can't see it
  • It doesn't answer the search
  • There isn't enough of it
  • Nobody vouches for it
  • The order matters

Almost every conversation about a website that "doesn't come up on Google" ends in the same place: the site isn't broken, it's just answering a question nobody asked, or it's answering it in a way Google can't read.

Before spending money on SEO, work through these four in order. Each one makes the next worth doing.

1. Google can't see it properly

This is the least glamorous problem and by far the most common. If a search engine can't crawl, render and index your pages quickly, nothing else you do matters.

Things that cause it:

  • Content loaded entirely by JavaScript after the page arrives
  • A slow site — particularly on a mid-range phone on 4G, which is how most people will actually see it
  • Pages accidentally blocked in robots.txt, or carrying a stray noindex tag left over from a staging site
  • No sitemap, or one that lists pages that no longer exist
  • Multiple versions of the same page — with and without www, with and without a trailing slash — and no canonical tag telling Google which one counts

Open Google Search Console and look at the Pages report. If your important pages aren't listed as indexed, stop everything else and fix that first. It's free, it takes an afternoon, and it's the difference between competing badly and not competing at all. It's also the first thing we check on any SEO project.

2. It doesn't answer the search anybody makes

The second most common problem is a site organised around how the business thinks rather than how customers search.

A clinic lists "musculoskeletal rehabilitation". People search "back pain physio near me". A studio offers "brand activation solutions". People search "product photographer Bournemouth". Both sites are honest. Neither will rank, because the words on the page and the words in the search box never meet.

Write down the ten sentences a customer would type immediately before they'd be ready to pay you. If you don't have a page that is obviously the best answer to each one, that's your content plan.

Note the phrase obviously the best answer. Not "mentions the keyword". Google is comfortably good enough now to tell the difference between a page about a subject and a page that actually resolves the question.

3. There isn't enough of it

A five-page website can rank beautifully for five things. It cannot rank for fifty. If you sell six services across four towns, that's potentially twenty-four distinct searches, and one "Services" page will not cover them.

This is where the work is, and it's why SEO takes months rather than weeks. Each page needs to justify existing on its own: a genuine answer, written once, properly. Twenty-four thin pages spun from a template are worse than nothing — they're the exact pattern search engines have spent a decade learning to discount.

A realistic pace for a small business is two to four proper pages a month. That's slow. It's also cumulative, permanent, and doesn't stop working the moment you stop paying for it. If the site itself is fighting you on speed or structure, that's a build problem rather than a content one.

4. Nobody else vouches for it

Links and mentions from other credible sites remain a significant ranking factor, and they're the hardest part to shortcut honestly.

What works for most small businesses isn't outreach campaigns. It's the ordinary stuff: local press, trade bodies and directories that actually matter in your industry, suppliers and partners who'll list you, sponsorships, and being genuinely useful enough that people cite you. Consistent business details across every listing — same name, same address, same phone number — matters more than most people expect, particularly for local search.

The order genuinely matters

Most SEO money gets spent at step four while step one is still broken. It's the equivalent of advertising a shop with the shutters down.

  1. Make sure the site is fast, crawlable and correctly indexed.
  2. Restructure it around what people actually search for.
  3. Publish steadily until you cover the whole question space.
  4. Then, and only then, worry about who links to you.

If you do the first three well, a surprising number of businesses never need to think hard about the fourth.

What a content retainer actually includes

Most of the value isn't in the posts. It's in the part that stops you needing to think about it.

8 Sept 2026  ·  5 min read

In this article

  • The per-post myth
  • What's really in the month
  • Where the time goes
  • Questions to ask

"Twelve posts a month" is how most content retainers get sold, and it's the least useful way to think about one. It's a bit like buying a restaurant meal by weight.

The per-post myth

Judging a retainer by output count pushes everyone towards the wrong behaviour. The supplier optimises for volume, because that's what's being measured. You get twelve forgettable things instead of four good ones, and nobody can say the contract wasn't honoured.

The businesses that get real value from content retainers buy something different: a system that runs whether or not anyone is feeling creative that week, and a person who already knows the business well enough to make decisions without a briefing every time. That's what a content strategy is actually for.

What's really in a month

A well-run monthly retainer usually contains most of this, whatever the headline number of posts says:

  • Planning. A calendar built a month ahead, mapped against what's actually happening in the business — launches, seasons, events, quiet periods.
  • Production. Shooting, writing, designing and editing the assets. Usually batched, because five things made in one session cost far less than five made separately.
  • Approval. Sending it to you, taking feedback, making changes. This is real work and it's always underestimated.
  • Publishing. Scheduling, captions, tags, links, formats per platform, checking it went out correctly.
  • Community. Replying to comments and messages, which is where most of the actual business conversations start.
  • Reporting. What went out, what performed, what's changing next month, and honestly which numbers don't mean anything.
  • Availability. Being reachable when something unexpected happens and you need a response by Thursday.

Where the time actually goes

People are often surprised that making the content is rarely the largest share of a retainer. Planning, approvals, publishing admin, community and reporting together tend to outweigh production time — and they're precisely the parts a business owner most wants off their desk. It's the bulk of what social media management actually involves.

You aren't buying twelve posts. You're buying the guarantee that twelve posts happen without you thinking about them.

This is also why the cheapest quote frequently costs more. If the price only covers production, planning and approvals land back with you, and the thing you were trying to outsource is still on your to-do list.

Questions worth asking before you sign

  1. Who does the actual work? If you're pitched by a strategist and delivered by a junior, that's fine — but you should know it now.
  2. What's the notice period? Anything longer than thirty days is protecting the supplier, not you.
  3. Who owns the files? You should get the raw footage and originals, not just exported crops.
  4. What happens in a slow month? Does unused time roll over, or evaporate?
  5. What does reporting look like? Ask to see a real, anonymised example. Screenshots of follower counts aren't reporting.
  6. What's explicitly excluded? Paid media, ad budget, travel, licensing and stock are the usual surprises.

A supplier who answers all six clearly and without defensiveness is telling you a lot about how the next twelve months will go.
